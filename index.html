Compass constructions in conformal geometric algebra

Ian Cavén, with Claude Opus 5.5 · 2026-11-07

Every classical construction step has a direct CGA form: a straightedge is a join with n_\infty, a compass is a dual sphere whose radius is carried by an inner product, and an intersection is a meet that returns a point pair. This page collects how far that goes: regular polygons by Carlyle circles in CGA2D, circles as cuts of lifted spheres in CGA3D, the icosahedron, and what can and cannot be reached.

Every construction starts from two given points, O and V_0. Euclidean coordinates are read only to pick one point of an intersection pair. The four demos are live ganja.js pages; each step lists the CGA operations it actually ran, recorded by the construction helpers.

New to geometric algebra? Section 1 introduces what the rest of the page uses, assuming only vectors and the dot product, with small figures to drag.

1Geometric algebra in brief

This section is for readers who know vectors and the dot product but have not met geometric algebra (GA). It builds up just enough to read the rest of the page: one new product, reflections and rotations as "sandwiches", and the conformal model in which points, circles and lines all become vectors. If GA is familiar, skip to section 2.

One product for vectors

For two vectors a and b, the geometric product is

ab = a\cdot b + a\wedge b .

The first part is the familiar dot product, a number. The second, the outer or wedge product, is a new kind of object, a bivector: the oriented parallelogram spanned by a and b, with area \lvert a\rvert\lvert b\rvert\sin\theta. It is antisymmetric, a\wedge b = -\,b\wedge a, so a\wedge a = 0.

With orthonormal basis vectors e_1, e_2: e_1e_1 = 1, and e_1e_2 = e_1\wedge e_2 = -\,e_2e_1, written e_{12}. Objects of different grade (scalars are grade 0, vectors 1, bivectors 2, and so on) are added side by side, the way a complex number adds a real and an imaginary part. The wedge of k vectors is a k-blade; it stands for a k-dimensional subspace with an orientation and a size. One identity explains why rotations will appear: e_{12}^2 = e_1e_2e_1e_2 = -\,e_1e_1e_2e_2 = -1.

Reflections and rotors

For a unit vector n, the "sandwich" x \mapsto -\,n\,x\,n reflects x in the line (or plane) perpendicular to n. Reflect in a, then in b:

x \;\mapsto\; b\,a\,x\,a\,b = R\,x\,\tilde R, \qquad R = ba,\quad \tilde R = ab .

Two reflections make a rotation by twice the angle from a to b. R is a rotor and \tilde R, its reverse, is R with the order of factors reversed; for a rotation by \theta, R = \cos\tfrac\theta2 - \sin\tfrac\theta2\, e_{12}. The same sandwich rotates any object, not just vectors. The polygon pages build their rotor exactly this way: R = (V_0 - V_1)\,L_x^{*} is a reflection in the line OV_0 followed by one in the bisector of V_0V_1.

Duality

The same subspace can be described in two ways: by the vectors that span it (an outer product), or by what is perpendicular to it (an inner product). Multiplying by the pseudoscalar, the unit blade of highest grade, converts one description into the other; this is the dual, written X^{*} (and !X in ganja.js). In the plane, the dual of a line's direction is its normal.

The conformal model: two extra dimensions

Plain vectors describe directions through an origin. Points elsewhere, translations and circles are not subspaces, so on their own they are awkward. Conformal geometric algebra (CGA; see Dorst, Fontijne and Mann 2007 for a full treatment) adds two basis vectors, e_+ with e_+^2 = 1 and e_- with e_-^2 = -1, and combines them into two null vectors:

n_\infty = e_- + e_+,\qquad n_o = \tfrac12(e_- - e_+),\qquad n_\infty^2 = n_o^2 = 0,\qquad n_o\cdot n_\infty = -1 .

n_o stands for the origin and n_\infty for the point at infinity. A Euclidean point x becomes the vector

P = n_o + x + \tfrac12 x^2\, n_\infty, \qquad P\cdot P = 0, \qquad P\cdot Q = -\tfrac12\,\lvert p - q\rvert^2 .

The last identity is the heart of everything on this page: a squared distance is now an inner product, a linear operation. That is why compass constructions turn into algebra.

Circles and lines are vectors too

Since X\cdot C = -\tfrac12\lvert x - c\rvert^2, the vector s = C - \tfrac12 r^2 n_\infty satisfies X\cdot s = 0 exactly for the points at distance r from C: it is the circle. A line with unit normal n at distance d from the origin is n + d\,n_\infty, a circle of infinite radius. Dually, by what spans them:

\text{circle through } A, B, C:\ A\wedge B\wedge C, \qquad \text{line through } A, B:\ A\wedge B\wedge n_\infty .

A line is a circle through infinity. Translations and scalings also become sandwiches with versors built from n_\infty, so one product covers all the Euclidean motions.

Join and meet

The outer product joins: A\wedge B\wedge C is the smallest circle containing the three points. The meet X\vee Y (& in ganja.js) intersects. Two circles, or a circle and a line, meet in a point pair T, a single object holding both intersection points, which section 2 splits into the two points. If the objects do not meet, the pair is imaginary, which shows as T\cdot T < 0.

2The CGA dictionary

In the plane (\mathbb{R}^{3,1}, with n_\infty = e_4 + e_3 and n_o = \tfrac12(e_4 - e_3) in ganja's convention), a point is

P(x) = n_o + x + \tfrac12 x^2 n_\infty, \qquad -P\cdot n_\infty = 1, \qquad P\cdot Q = -\tfrac12\,\lvert p - q\rvert^2 .

The last identity is what makes compass work algebraic: a squared distance between two points is an inner product, so a length can be transferred without ever being measured.

Straightedge
L_{AB} = A \wedge B \wedge n_\infty
Compass
The circle centred at C with radius \lvert P_1P_2\rvert is \big(C + (P_1\cdot P_2)\,n_\infty\big)^{*}, since its dual is C - \tfrac12 r^2 n_\infty. With P_1 = C it is the circle through P_2.
Intersection
The meet T = X \vee Y of two lines or circles is a point pair, split by P_\pm = \big(T \pm \sqrt{T\cdot T}\big)\,(n_\infty \rfloor T); T\cdot T < 0 means the objects do not meet.
Bisection
Classically, with two circles: T_{AB} = C_A(B) \vee C_B(A), bisector B_{AB} = T_{AB} \wedge n_\infty, midpoint M = \operatorname{pt}(B_{AB} \vee L_{AB}).
Flat point to point
Two lines meet in F = M \wedge n_\infty. With Y = n_o \rfloor F normalized, M = Y + \tfrac12 (Y\cdot Y)\, n_\infty.
Centre of a circle
The centre is s\, n_\infty\, s, where s = C^{*} is the circle's dual vector. In words, it is the point at infinity reflected in the circle.
Rotor
R = (V_0 - V_1)\, L_x^{*}, normalized: a reflection in the line OV_0, then in the bisector of V_0V_1 (the vector V_0 - V_1), which passes through O. Rotor stepping is \hat V_{k+1} = R\,\hat V_k\,\tilde R.
Compass stepping
C_k = \big(V_k + (V_0\cdot V_1)\,n_\infty\big)^{*} meets the unit circle in V_{k-1} and V_{k+1}; the next vertex is the one farther from V_{k-1}, i.e. with the larger -X\cdot V_{k-1}.
Branch choice
The only Euclidean readout: for a normalized point, x = P\cdot e_1 and y = P\cdot e_2, used to take the larger root or the upper point of a pair.
Two ganja.js details matter here. Inside Algebra(…), !x is the dual, not logical negation, so boolean tests are written === false. And a literal such as 1e-10 is read as a basis blade, so tolerances are written 1E-10.

3Regular polygons by Carlyle circles

Gauss's construction of the 17-gon (Gauss 1801) solves a tower of quadratics in the Gauss periods \eta. Carlyle circles turn each quadratic into a single circle; the constructions here follow DeTemple 1991. For a quadratic x^2 - s\,x + p = 0, the Carlyle circle has diameter A = (0,1) to B = (s,p) and meets the x-axis at the two roots. Its centre is

M = \big(\tfrac{s}{2},\, \tfrac{1+p}{2}\big) = \operatorname{midpoint}\big(S = (s,0),\; Y = (0,\, 1+p)\big),

so each Carlyle step only needs a point S on the x-axis and a point Y on the y-axis, and the circle is C_M(A). What differs between divisions is how S and Y come from earlier roots.

The frame

From O and V_0: the x-axis O\wedge V_0\wedge n_\infty, the unit circle C_O(V_0), their second meet Q = (-1,0), the y-axis as the bisector of QV_0, and A = (0,1) where it meets the unit circle.

The final step

Every division ends at H = (2\cos\tfrac{2\pi}{n},\,0). The circle at H with radius \lvert OV_0\rvert meets the unit circle at V_1 and V_{n-1}, whose x-coordinate is \cos\tfrac{2\pi}{n}; then rotor and compass stepping.

The 17-gon

The steps follow DeTemple's construction of the 17-gon by four Carlyle circles (DeTemple 1991).

  • Periods of length 8: x^2 + x - 4 = 0. Its centre A_0 = (-\tfrac12,-\tfrac32) is where the circle at Q' through V_0 (radius \tfrac32) meets the bisector of QO. Roots A_1 = \eta_{0,2}, A_2 = \eta_{1,2}.
  • Length 4: \eta_{0,4}+\eta_{2,4} = \eta_{0,2}, \eta_{0,4}\eta_{2,4} = -1, and likewise for \eta_{1,2}. With p = -1, Y = O, so the centres are the midpoints of OA_1 and OA_2; the larger roots are A_5, A_6.
  • Length 2: \eta_{0,8} + \eta_{4,8} = \eta_{0,4}, \eta_{0,8}\eta_{4,8} = \eta_{1,4}. Here Y = (0,\,1+\eta_{1,4}): the compass opened to \lvert QA_6\rvert = 1 + \eta_{1,4} at Q and placed at O meets the y-axis at A_7. The centre A_8 bisects A_7A_5, and the larger root is A_9 = 2\cos\tfrac{2\pi}{17}.

The 15-gon (Euclid IV.16)

Since \tfrac25 - \tfrac13 = \tfrac1{15}, the triangle vertex U_1 (at 120°) and the second pentagon vertex P_2 (at 144°) are themselves 15-gon vertices, V_5 and V_6. The compass opened to \lvert U_1P_2\rvert, the circle C_{U_1}(P_2), is the side, and the polygon is stepped from that edge with R = (U_1 - P_2)\, L_{OU_1}^{*}. Nothing has to be transferred to V_0.

4Lifting spheres in CGA3D

In \mathbb{R}^{4,1}, take the construction plane \Pi_0 (in the demos ganja's floor, so heights point up on screen). A sphere of radius \rho centred at a point lifted by h above C cuts \Pi_0 in the circle centred at C with radius \sqrt{\rho^2 - h^2}.

Write e_h for the unit vector perpendicular to \Pi_0: the height direction, the plane's normal. (The h in its name stands for height; it is a label, not an operation.) In the demos e_h = e_2, ganja's vertical, and up to sign it is the plane's dual \Pi_0^{*}, the direction the vertical lines below use. The point at height h above C has Euclidean position c + h\,e_h, and since c lies in the plane, c\cdot e_h = 0, its squared length is c^2 + h^2, so

C' = n_o + c + h\,e_h + \tfrac12\big(c^2 + h^2\big)\, n_\infty = C + h\,e_h + \tfrac12 h^2\, n_\infty, \qquad C\cdot C' = -\tfrac12 h^2 .

Algebraically the lift is therefore one inner-product term: the cut is the in-plane circle

C + \big(P_1\cdot P_2 - C\cdot C'\big)\, n_\infty, \qquad P_1\cdot P_2 = -\tfrac12\rho^2 .

Lifting subtracts h^2 from the squared radius. The question is how to locate C' with CGA3D operations alone.

MethodConstructionPicks a given h?
1. Stand a length up Vertical line L^{\uparrow}_C = C\wedge\Pi_0^{*}\wedge n_\infty; C' = \operatorname{split}\big(L^{\uparrow}_C \vee S_C(\lvert P_1P_2\rvert)\big), higher point. Yes, directly
2. Trilateration Three spheres centred in \Pi_0 meet in a pair mirror-symmetric in \Pi_0: T = S_A\vee S_B\vee S_C. Its line T\wedge n_\infty is vertical. No, h is a by-product
3. Reflection (C - Q)\,X\,(C - Q) moves a lifted point X above Q to the same height above C. No, it reuses a height

A variant of method 1 fits a fixed sphere size: the vertical through Q meets the sphere of radius \rho at C at height \sqrt{\rho^2 - \lvert CQ\rvert^2}, which is Pythagoras done by a single meet.

The pentagon by a lifted sphere

The 5-gon's Carlyle circle has centre Q' and radius \lvert Q'A\rvert = \tfrac{\sqrt5}{2}, and by Pythagoras

\lvert Q'A\rvert^2 = \lvert Q'V_0\rvert^2 - \lvert OV_0\rvert^2 = \tfrac94 - 1 = \tfrac54 .

So the sphere of radius \lvert Q'V_0\rvert centred at Q' lifted by \lvert OV_0\rvert cuts \Pi_0 in exactly the Carlyle circle. Both lengths are already in the plane, so the lift is method 1. The rest carries over from 2D: S_A(B)\vee S_B(A) is a circle K whose carrier plane \beta = K\wedge n_\infty is the bisector plane, so \beta\vee\Pi_0 is the bisector and \operatorname{pt}(L_{AB}\vee\beta) the midpoint; circles meet in the plane as S_1\vee S_2\vee\Pi_0; and the rotor reflects in vertical planes, R = (V_0 - V_1)\,\Pi_x^{*} with \Pi_x = L_x\wedge\Pi_0^{*}.

5Platonic solids

Each 2D tool has a 3D counterpart, which gives three routes to the vertices of a solid:

  • Euclid XIII with method 1. Plane constructions plus lengths stood up on perpendiculars.
  • Trilateration stepping, the 3D compass stepping: a vertex adjacent to known A and B is \operatorname{split}\big(S_u\vee S_A(e)\vee S_B(e)\big), for edge length e (relative to the circumradius: tetrahedron \sqrt{8/3}, octahedron \sqrt2, cube 2/\sqrt3, icosahedron 4/\sqrt{10+2\sqrt5}, dodecahedron 4/\big(\sqrt3(1+\sqrt5)\big)).
  • Reflection groups, the 3D rotor stepping: the vertices are the orbit of a seed point under A_3, B_3 or H_3, with each mirror plane through O a vector, as V_0 - V_1 already is (on H_3 in Clifford algebra, see Dechant 2014).

All five by route 1

Every vertex comes from the same stand-up: a line through a point C meets a sphere centred at C, and the point pair gives both signs, so one meet yields two vertices. The lengths stood up are \lvert OV_0\rvert = 1 and, for the icosahedron and dodecahedron, \lvert OH'\rvert = \varphi and \lvert OH\rvert = 1/\varphi: the pentagon construction's Carlyle roots are H = 1/\varphi and H' = -\varphi. For every solid the edges are the vertex pairs at the minimum distance, read from the inner products I_i\cdot I_j = -\tfrac12 e^2. The faces are the planes \pi = I_i\wedge I_j\wedge I_k\wedge n_\infty through adjacent vertices that leave every vertex on one side, the sign of X\wedge\pi: triangles, squares or pentagons alike. Radial projection then fits the unit sphere: J_k is the point of L_{OI_k}\vee S_u nearer I_k, a uniform scaling (for the icosahedron by 1/\sqrt{1+\varphi^2}, giving the edge 4/\sqrt{10+2\sqrt5}\approx 1.0515).

Building the vertical lines

C\wedge\Pi_0^{*}\wedge n_\infty reads the perpendicular off the plane's dual. Two classical alternatives build it from spheres; the demo can show any of the three.

  • Two bisector planes (Euclid XI.11–12). The circle of radius 1 around the base point meets the floor line at D_1, D_2; their bisector plane \beta_1 is vertical and cuts the floor in the perpendicular, which meets the circle at D_3, D_4; their bisector plane is \beta_2, and L^{\uparrow} = \beta_1\vee\beta_2.
  • Three spheres. Spheres of equal radius around three points of a circle in \Pi_0 meet in a pair T^{\uparrow} on its axis, so L^{\uparrow} = T^{\uparrow}\wedge n_\infty, the 3D counterpart of B_{AB} = T_{AB}\wedge n_\infty. With radius \sqrt2 around points of the unit circle about G, the pair sits at height \sqrt{2-1} = 1: it is the icosahedron's vertices (\varphi, 0, \pm1) themselves.

6What can be constructed

Why 3D adds nothing new

Every operation on these pages is an intersection of lines, planes and spheres:

  • Planes and lines give linear equations.
  • Two spheres \lvert x - a\rvert^2 = r^2 and \lvert x - b\rvert^2 = s^2 subtract to a linear equation, the radical plane. That is the bisector plane \beta that the bisection builds.
  • So any meet involving spheres comes down to linear equations plus a single quadratic.

Each construction step therefore adds at most one square root, and lifting a sphere adds nothing more: its cut has radius \sqrt{\rho^2 - h^2}. A length or coordinate is constructible with these tools exactly when it lies in a tower of quadratic extensions of \mathbb{Q}, the same field as plane constructions (Wantzel 1837). A third dimension, spheres, lifts and trilateration change how short or elegant a construction is, never what it can reach.

Polygons: the same list as Gauss–Wantzel

A regular n-gon is constructible exactly when n = 2^k\, p_1\cdots p_m with the p_i distinct Fermat primes (3, 5, 17, 257, 65537). In 3D this still rules out the 7-gon and the 9-gon.

From that it follows that the orbit of a constructible point under T, O or I is always constructible, and under Cₙ or Dₙ exactly when the n-gon is. I'm not aware of a named theorem stating this, but it follows directly from the field argument above.

Some consequences:

  • All five Platonic solids are constructible (Euclid, Book XIII). Their coordinates need only \sqrt2, \sqrt3 and \sqrt5.
  • All Archimedean solids are constructible except the snub cube and the snub dodecahedron. The snub cube's vertices are the even permutations of (\pm1, \pm1/t, \pm t) with an odd number of minus signs, where t\approx 1.839 is the tribonacci constant, a root of the irreducible cubic t^3 - t^2 - t - 1 = 0. Since t is a ratio of lengths, no scaling helps. The snub dodecahedron needs a root of a cubic over \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt5).
  • Some Johnson solids fail the same way; the snub disphenoid's coordinates satisfy an irreducible cubic.

Adding one non-quadratic tool (angle trisection, neusis, a conic, origami folds) changes the answer. Gleason (1988) showed that with an angle trisector the n-gon is constructible exactly when n = 2^r 3^s p_1\cdots p_m with distinct Pierpont primes p_i = 2^u 3^v + 1 > 3 (7, 13, 19, 37, 73, …). That brings in the 7-gon and the 9-gon, and its cube roots reach the snub solids.

7Beyond square roots: a parabola

Descartes' method (La Géométrie, 1637) solves any cubic or quartic with one fixed parabola y = x^2 and circles. Substituting into x^2 + y^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0 gives

x^4 + (1 + E)\,x^2 + D\,x + F = 0 .

For a depressed cubic t^3 + pt + q = 0, multiply by t: the quartic t^4 + pt^2 + qt needs E = p - 1, D = q, F = 0, so the circle passes through O with centre

\Big(-\frac{q}{2},\ \frac{1 - p}{2}\Big),

built from the cubic's coefficients by ordinary constructions. It meets the parabola at the origin and at x = t for each real root.

Any fixed non-degenerate conic would do (Pappus trisected with a rectangular hyperbola, Menaechmus doubled the cube with two parabolas); with one, compass and straightedge reach the towers of degree-2 and degree-3 extensions, the same field as origami.

Then X' = L_x^{*} X L_x^{*} reflects it in the x-axis, the line XX' meets the axis at H = (2\cos 40^\circ, 0), and the usual final step and rotor finish the 9-gon.

8Open directions

UNVERIFIED. The descriptions in this section were written from memory: check the basis and sign conventions against the sources before relying on them. The publication details in the references have been checked.

Conic space, ℝ⁶ (Perwass)

Six is the smallest dimension in which every conic ax^2 + by^2 + 2hxy + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0 is a vector. Perwass (2009) builds conic space on the symmetric 3\times3 matrices with the trace inner product:

  • A conic is the vector M = \begin{pmatrix} a & h & g\\ h & b & f\\ g & f & c\end{pmatrix}.
  • A point is X = \hat x\hat x^{\mathsf T}, \hat x = (x, y, 1), the Veronese embedding: (x^2, y^2, \sqrt2\,xy, \sqrt2\,x, \sqrt2\,y, 1) in an orthonormal basis.
  • Incidence is the inner product, X\cdot M = \hat x^{\mathsf T} M \hat x, and the outer product of five points gives the dual of the conic through them.
  • The parabola y = x^2 is a = 1, f = -\tfrac12, all else zero. With a circle C, the intersections satisfy X\rfloor(P\wedge C) = 0: the pencil carries them.

Two limits compared with CGA: valid points must lie on the Veronese surface, so the four intersections cannot be read off by factorizing a blade (extracting them is the quartic itself); and not every Euclidean motion is a versor, translations in particular.

GAC, ℝ5,3

The geometric algebra for conics of Hrdina, Návrat and Vašík (2018) keeps conics as vectors and restores translations, rotations and scalings as versors, with the plane's CGA \mathbb{R}^{3,1} inside it. A 9-gon construction in the style of these pages (meets, sandwiches, rotors, plus a parabola) fits there.

Other 6D algebras

  • \mathbb{R}^{5,1}, the CGA of 4D space: hyperspheres and hyperplanes; a parabola is not a native element.
  • \mathbb{R}^{4,2}, Lie sphere geometry of the plane (or Plücker line geometry of 3D): oriented circles, lines and points with tangency as orthogonality; a parabola appears only indirectly, as an envelope.

Inside CGA itself there is a hint: the embedding n_o + x\,e_1 + \tfrac12 x^2 n_\infty traces \tfrac12 x^2 in its n_\infty coefficient, so the point embedding already is a parabola, but the algebra cannot meet it with an arbitrary circle. That step from quadratics to quartics is what the larger algebras add.
